Comprehensive Clinical Guide: A1C & Average Glucose

What Is an A1C Test?

The hemoglobin A1C test (also referred to as HbA1c, glycated hemoglobin, or glycohemoglobin) is a routine laboratory blood test that evaluates your average blood glucose levels over the preceding 2 to 3 months. When glucose travels through your bloodstream, a portion naturally binds to hemoglobin, the primary oxygen-carrying protein inside your red blood cells. Because red blood cells survive for an average of 120 days before being recycled, the proportion of glycated hemoglobin provides an accurate biological record of cumulative glucose exposure.

What Is Estimated Average Glucose (eAG)?

Estimated Average Glucose (eAG) translates your A1C percentage into the familiar units used by home fingerstick meters and Continuous Glucose Monitors (CGMs)—specifically milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL) in the United States and millimoles per liter (mmol/L) in the United Kingdom. Developed by the international A1C-Derived Average Glucose (ADAG) clinical trial, eAG allows individuals with diabetes to directly correlate laboratory percentages with day-to-day blood sugar measurements.

A1C vs. Daily Blood Sugar Readings

A single fingerstick glucose reading reflects your glucose level at one discrete second in time. It responds rapidly to food intake, physical exertion, illness, hormonal fluctuations, and medication. By contrast, an A1C reading represents a mathematically weighted average of all your fasting, pre-meal, post-meal, and sleeping glucose levels across hundreds of hours.

How the A1C Conversion Formula Works

The mathematical relationship standardized by the ADAG study and endorsed by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) is defined as:
eAG (mg/dL) = (28.7 × A1C) − 46.7
To convert from an established average blood sugar back to an estimated A1C percentage, the reverse algebraic equation is applied:
A1C (%) = (eAG in mg/dL + 46.7) ÷ 28.7

Understanding mg/dL and mmol/L

In the United States, blood sugar concentrations are reported in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL). In the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and many international regions, glucose is quantified in millimoles per liter (mmol/L). The direct conversion relationship between the two systems is exactly 18.0:
mmol/L = mg/dL ÷ 18.0  |  mg/dL = mmol/L × 18.0

Understanding NGSP (%) and IFCC (mmol/mol) Units

The National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program (NGSP) expresses A1C as a percentage (e.g., 7.0%). In contrast, the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) standardizes HbA1c as millimoles of glycated hemoglobin per mole of unglycated hemoglobin (mmol/mol). The standard conversion formula is:
IFCC (mmol/mol) = (A1C − 2.152) ÷ 0.09148

Why A1C and Glucose Results May Not Match

Occasionally, an individual's laboratory A1C result does not mirror their home blood glucose average. Discrepancies often occur when fingerstick testing misses asymptomatic nighttime lows or postprandial sugar spikes. Furthermore, individual biological differences in red blood cell lifespan can shift the glycation rate higher or lower than population averages.

Important A1C Test Limitations

Because the A1C test depends on hemoglobin physiology, certain medical conditions can alter results independently of actual blood glucose levels:

  • Anemia: Iron-deficiency anemia can falsely elevate A1C readings, whereas hemolytic anemia accelerates cell destruction and falsely lowers A1C.
  • Blood loss or transfusion: Recent major hemorrhage or blood transfusions replace older red blood cells with new ones, resulting in falsely low A1C values.
  • Pregnancy: Altered erythropoiesis during the second and third trimesters can distort A1C accuracy.
  • Kidney and liver disease: Chronic kidney disease, hemodialysis, and cirrhosis alter red cell survival and hemoglobin structure.
  • Hemoglobin variants: Genetic traits such as sickle cell trait (HbS), HbC, HbE, or thalassemia can interfere with specific laboratory assay methods.
  • Supplements: High-dose vitamin C or vitamin E supplementation may interfere with test chemistry.

When to Discuss Results With a Healthcare Professional

If your home glucose records and laboratory A1C results show unexpected differences, consult a qualified physician or endocrinologist. They can order secondary glycemic assessments, such as serum fructosamine tests or professional CGM sensor reports, to confirm your metabolic status.
