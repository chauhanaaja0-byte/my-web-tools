Understanding Overtime Pay & U.S. Wage Standards

What Is Overtime Pay?

Overtime pay is financial compensation paid to eligible employees for hours worked beyond a specified threshold during a defined work period. In the United States, the federal baseline establishes that nonexempt workers receive extra remuneration when their weekly working hours exceed 40 hours in a single, 168-hour workweek. Overtime serves both as fair compensation for extended labor and as an incentive for employers to maintain manageable schedules for their workforce.

How Does an Overtime Pay Calculator Work?

An overtime pay calculator applies standard payroll arithmetic to allocate an employee's total logged hours into regular straight-time hours and overtime hours. Once divided, the tool multiplies regular hours by the base hourly wage and overtime hours by the adjusted overtime rate (such as 1.5× base wage). Finally, it combines both amounts to calculate total gross pay before deductions for income taxes, Social Security, Medicare, or elective benefits.

How to Calculate Overtime Pay

Calculating overtime pay requires four simple steps:

  • Step 1: Identify your regular base hourly rate (for example, $20.00 per hour).
  • Step 2: Determine your overtime multiplier. Federal time-and-a-half corresponds to a 1.5 multiplier ($20.00 × 1.5 = $30.00 overtime rate).
  • Step 3: Calculate your overtime hours by subtracting your threshold (usually 40) from your total hours worked (e.g., 45 total hours − 40 = 5 overtime hours).
  • Step 4: Multiply your overtime hours by your overtime rate (5 hours × $30.00 = $150.00 overtime pay).

What Is Time and a Half?

“Time and a half” is the common American term for overtime pay computed at 150% (1.5 times) an employee’s regular hourly rate. For example, if an employee earns $24.00 per hour in regular straight time, their time-and-a-half rate equals $36.00 per hour ($24.00 + $12.00).

How to Calculate an Overtime Rate

To calculate an overtime rate, multiply your regular hourly rate by the agreed or statutory multiplier. For 1.5×, multiply by 1.5. For double time (2.0×), multiply by 2. If you earn $18.50 per hour, your standard 1.5× overtime rate is $27.75 per hour ($18.50 × 1.5).

How to Calculate Regular Pay

Regular pay represents straight-time wages earned up to the overtime threshold. If you work 45 hours at $20.00 per hour with a 40-hour threshold, your regular hours equal 40. Your regular pay is calculated as 40 hours × $20.00 = $800.00.

How to Calculate Gross Pay With Overtime

Gross pay with overtime represents the sum of regular wages plus overtime wages. In our previous example, $800.00 regular pay plus $150.00 overtime pay equals $950.00 in total weekly gross earnings before taxes and payroll withholdings.

What Is an Overtime Premium?

The overtime premium is the incremental dollar amount paid above standard straight-time compensation for overtime hours. For instance, if an employee works 5 overtime hours at a base rate of $20.00 with a 1.5× multiplier, their total overtime pay is $150.00. However, because those 5 hours already have a straight-time value of $100.00 (5 × $20.00), the extra “premium” earned specifically due to overtime is $50.00 (5 × $20.00 × 0.5).

How Does the 40-Hour Workweek Work?

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), the statutory workweek is a fixed, regularly recurring period of 168 hours—comprising seven consecutive 24-hour periods. Overtime is calculated based on hours physically worked within that specific workweek, regardless of pay period frequency.

Can Saturday Hours Be Overtime?

Federal law does not automatically designate Saturday work as overtime simply because it falls on a weekend. Saturday hours count toward the cumulative weekly total. If working on Saturday pushes your total hours for the workweek above 40, those hours above 40 are paid at overtime rates. However, union contracts or specific employer policies may offer premium pay for weekend shifts.

Can Sunday Hours Be Overtime?

Similarly, federal wage regulations do not require extra pay for work on Sundays unless the employee has already exceeded 40 hours for that workweek. While some state laws or collective bargaining agreements provide special weekend premiums, federal FLSA baseline rules rely on total weekly hours.

Does Working on a Holiday Automatically Mean Overtime?

Federal law does not require overtime pay simply for working on holidays like Thanksgiving, Christmas, Memorial Day, or Labor Day. Holiday pay is a matter of agreement between an employer and an employee (or their union). However, if holiday hours cause the total weekly hours worked to exceed 40, overtime pay is required for the excess hours.

What Is a Workweek?

A workweek is any established, recurring seven-day period chosen by the employer. It does not have to match the calendar week (Sunday through Saturday). It can begin on any day and at any hour (such as Wednesday at 12:01 AM and end Tuesday at midnight). Once established, it remains fixed unless an intentional, permanent change is made.

Why Two Workweeks Should Not Be Averaged for Federal Weekly Overtime

A critical rule of federal overtime compliance is that each workweek stands completely on its own. Employers may not average hours worked across two separate weeks in a biweekly pay period. For example, if an employee works 50 hours in Week 1 and 30 hours in Week 2 (totaling 80 hours across two weeks), the employer cannot claim zero overtime was earned. The employee is legally entitled to 10 hours of overtime pay for Week 1.

Advanced Overtime Concepts, Payroll Rules & Best Practices

How Decimal Hours Work

Modern payroll systems calculate compensation using decimal hours rather than hours and minutes. In decimal format, fractions of an hour correspond to minutes divided by 60. For example, 15 minutes is 0.25 hours, 30 minutes is 0.5 hours, and 45 minutes is 0.75 hours.

Hours and Minutes vs. Decimal Hours

A common error is confusing clock minutes with decimal hours. For instance, 8 hours and 30 minutes is 8.5 hours, not 8.30 hours. Entering 8.30 would undercount your shift by 12 minutes (0.30 × 60 = 18 minutes). Always use the exact decimal equivalent when computing wages.

How to Calculate a Weekly Timesheet

To accurately complete a weekly timesheet, record your start time, break deductions, and end time for each day from Monday through Sunday. Sum the daily net hours to reach your weekly total. Under federal guidelines, subtract 40 hours from that total to determine overtime eligibility.

What Is Double-Time Pay?

Double-time pay is compensation calculated at twice (2.0×) an employee’s regular base wage rate. While federal FLSA does not mandate double-time pay, certain state laws (such as California under specific conditions exceeding 12 daily hours or on the 7th consecutive workday) or company union agreements require double-time compensation for extended shifts or holiday schedules.

Can Employers Use Different Overtime Multipliers?

Yes. Federal law sets 1.5× as a mandatory minimum baseline for covered nonexempt employees. Employers are legally permitted to offer higher multipliers, such as 1.75×, 2.0×, or 2.5×, as part of employment agreements, collective bargaining contracts, or incentive programs, but they cannot legally pay less than 1.5× when federal overtime applies.

Federal vs. State Overtime Rules

Some states enforce overtime rules that are more protective than federal guidelines. For example, California, Alaska, and Nevada enforce daily overtime thresholds (such as overtime after 8 hours worked in a single day) in addition to weekly limits. Under American labor law, when federal and state laws conflict, the standard that provides greater protection and higher pay to the employee always prevails.

Salaried Employees and Overtime

Being paid on a salary basis does not automatically make an employee exempt from overtime. To be exempt under federal executive, administrative, or professional exemptions, an employee must satisfy specific salary level requirements and specific job duties tests. Nonexempt salaried employees are still entitled to overtime pay when they work more than 40 hours per week.

Exempt vs. Nonexempt Employees

Nonexempt employees are protected by FLSA regulations, must be paid at least the federal minimum wage, and must receive overtime pay for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. Exempt employees are excluded from overtime protections, generally because they meet specific executive, administrative, professional, or outside sales criteria established by the Department of Labor.

Why Regular Rate Can Differ From Base Hourly Rate

In federal labor law, the “regular rate” is not always simply the base hourly rate shown on your pay stub. It is defined as total remuneration for employment divided by total hours worked in that workweek. When workers receive nondiscretionary bonuses, shift differentials, or performance commissions, those additional payments must often be factored back into the regular rate, resulting in a higher overtime pay rate.

Bonuses and Overtime

Nondiscretionary bonuses (such as attendance bonuses, production incentives, or quality awards) are considered part of an employee’s regular compensation under the FLSA. When earned during a workweek that includes overtime, the bonus amount must be apportioned across the hours worked to recompute the true regular rate and provide additional overtime back-pay.

Multiple Pay Rates

When an employee performs two or more different jobs for the same employer at different pay rates during the same workweek, their regular rate for overtime is typically calculated using the weighted average of all rates (or by an agreed-upon rate in effect during overtime hours if established in advance).

Overtime Pay vs. Take-Home Pay

It is important to remember that this calculator estimates gross pay—the total amount earned before any deductions. Your net take-home pay on your actual paycheck will be lower due to mandatory payroll taxes, including federal income tax, state income tax, local taxes, FICA (Social Security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%), plus any voluntary pre-tax or post-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions or health insurance premiums.

How to Estimate Annual Income With Overtime

If you regularly work overtime, projecting your annual income can help you budget effectively. You can estimate your annual gross income by multiplying your average weekly gross earnings by 52 weeks. However, keep in mind that overtime availability may fluctuate depending on company demand, seasonality, and scheduling changes.

Common Overtime Calculation Mistakes

Some of the most frequent payroll mistakes include:

  • Failing to include nondiscretionary bonuses in the regular rate.
  • Averaging hours over a two-week pay period instead of calculating week-by-week.
  • Treating 8 hours 30 minutes as 8.30 decimal hours instead of 8.5 hours.
  • Assuming salaried employees are automatically exempt without verifying duty tests.
  • Failing to track short rest breaks under 20 minutes, which are compensable by law.

Frequently Asked Questions About Overtime Pay

What is overtime pay?
Overtime pay is extra compensation paid to eligible employees when they work beyond a designated number of hours—most commonly over 40 hours in a standard workweek in the United States.
How do I calculate overtime pay?
To calculate overtime pay, multiply your base hourly rate by your overtime multiplier (usually 1.5×) to find your overtime rate. Then multiply that overtime rate by the number of overtime hours worked.
What is time and a half?
Time and a half refers to a wage rate of 1.5 times an employee's normal hourly pay. For instance, an employee earning $20 per hour receives $30 per hour for overtime hours worked.
How do I calculate a 1.5 overtime rate?
Multiply your base hourly wage by 1.5. For example, $18.00 × 1.5 = $27.00 per hour.
What is my overtime rate at $15 an hour?
At $15.00 an hour, your standard 1.5× time-and-a-half overtime rate is $22.50 per hour ($15.00 × 1.5).
What is my overtime rate at $20 an hour?
At $20.00 an hour, your standard 1.5× time-and-a-half overtime rate is $30.00 per hour ($20.00 × 1.5).
What is my overtime rate at $25 an hour?
At $25.00 an hour, your standard 1.5× time-and-a-half overtime rate is $37.50 per hour ($25.00 × 1.5).
When does federal overtime generally start?
Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), overtime generally begins once a covered, nonexempt employee works more than 40 hours in a single, defined workweek.
What counts as a workweek?
For federal overtime purposes, a workweek is a fixed, regularly recurring period of 168 consecutive hours (seven 24-hour periods). It can start on any calendar day.
Is overtime calculated daily or weekly?
Under federal FLSA rules, overtime is calculated on a weekly basis (over 40 hours). However, some states (like California, Alaska, and Nevada) also mandate daily overtime after 8 hours in a workday.
Does Saturday automatically count as overtime?
No. Federal law does not treat Saturday work as overtime unless working those hours brings your total hours for the workweek above the 40-hour threshold.
Does Sunday automatically count as overtime?
No. Sunday work does not automatically qualify as overtime under federal rules unless weekly hours exceed 40 or a specific employer policy or union agreement applies.
Are holidays automatically paid as overtime?
Federal law does not require extra overtime pay simply for working on a holiday. Any premium holiday pay is governed by employer agreements or union contracts.
What is regular pay?
Regular pay is your baseline straight-time compensation earned for hours worked up to the standard threshold (e.g., 40 regular hours × hourly rate).
What is overtime premium pay?
Overtime premium pay is the specific additional amount paid above your regular wage for working overtime hours (e.g., 0.5× hourly rate per overtime hour).
What is gross pay?
Gross pay is your total earned compensation (regular pay plus overtime and any bonuses) before taxes, insurance premiums, and payroll deductions are withheld.

What does exempt employee mean?
An exempt employee is someone excluded from FLSA overtime and minimum wage requirements, typically executive, administrative, professional, or outside sales workers.
